Ember · voice · proposed architecture

Split the ears and the mouth from the brain

ChatGPT's voice call feels like a person because one model hears the audio and speaks the reply — nothing is flattened into text in between, so it catches your hesitation, emits real prosody, and can be cut off mid-sentence. Ember can have that feel without moving your thinking to a model that doesn't know your business: OpenAI's Realtime API becomes the conversational layer, and Claude stays the brain.

The realtime model is a conversationalist, not an oracle. It owns listening, turn-taking and speech; the moment a question has substance it delegates. Claude never touches audio, so it keeps the full 1M context, your memory, and the panel.

02Why today's voice doesn't feel like that

It isn't the model. It's that four things happen in a row that should overlap.

Ember today

A walkie-talkie

Azure STT waits for you to finish, hands a final transcript to the prompt, Claude answers in full, then Azure TTS reads it out. Four stages, strictly serialized.

No barge-in — once it starts talking you wait. No prosody — the TTS is reading text written to be read. And it's narrating a coding agent's transcript, not holding a conversation.

Proposed

A phone call

Audio streams continuously. Server-side voice activity detection decides when you've stopped, not a silence timer. The model can acknowledge instantly while Claude is still thinking, and you can talk over it.

Claude's answer streams into speech sentence by sentence, so you hear the first clause while the last is still being written.

04One exchange, end to end

Including the two things that make it feel human: the instant acknowledgement, and being interruptible.

sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant You
    participant RT as Realtime layer
    participant E as Ember bridge
    participant C as Claude

    You->>RT: "so what should I do about Jendoo pricing"
    Note over RT: VAD: turn ended (~200ms)
    RT-->>You: "Let me think about that properly."
    RT->>E: ask_claude("Jendoo pricing — what should he do?")
    E->>C: full context: memory, ventures vault, history
    Note over C: thinking, ~4s
    C-->>E: streamed answer, first clause
    E-->>RT: tool result (streaming)
    RT-->>You: speaks it as it arrives
    C->>E: show_panel(pricing comparison)
    Note over You: table appears beside the terminal
    You->>RT: "wait — what about the annual tier"
    Note over RT: barge-in: stops mid-word
    RT->>E: ask_claude("annual tier?", same thread)

Step 3 is the whole trick. The acknowledgement costs nothing, buys four seconds, and is the difference between a pause that feels like thinking and one that feels like a dropped call. Step 12 is the other half — you can cut it off, which no turn-based pipeline allows.

The trap is precisely your use case

Realtime costs run above the per-minute math on long calls, because every turn re-processes the growing audio context. A two-hour business session is where that bites hardest — the naive version could be several times the sticker.

This is a second, independent reason for the split: the realtime layer keeps a short rolling context — the last few turns, enough for conversational continuity — while Claude carries the actual history. Long sessions then cost roughly what short ones do.

The honest risk

The seamless feel comes from one model doing everything. Every hop out to Claude reintroduces latency on exactly the turns you care most about. The acknowledgement trick hides it well — but whether "well" is good enough is a thing you have to hear, not reason about. That's the entire argument for a throwaway spike before any of this touches Ember properly.
